Peptide Solubility:
Which Research Compounds Dissolve How

North Peptides UK · 7 min read · Research Use Only

One of the most practical questions in peptide research preparation is how a given compound will dissolve, and in what solvent. Most research peptides stocked by North Peptides UK dissolve readily in bacteriostatic water, but solubility varies with amino-acid composition, sequence length, and molecular charge. This guide covers the solubility characteristics of the most commonly handled research peptides, with a comparison table and troubleshooting notes for difficult preparations.

What Does Peptide Solubility Mean in Practice?

Solubility is the maximum amount of a compound that will dissolve in a given volume of solvent at a specified temperature to form a stable, homogeneous solution. For research peptides, practical solubility determines the concentration that can be prepared and — critically — which solvent must be used.

Most research peptides are fairly hydrophilic (water-loving) and dissolve adequately in aqueous solvents such as bacteriostatic water or normal saline. However, peptides with a high proportion of hydrophobic (non-polar) amino-acid residues may require acidic, alkaline, or organic co-solvents to dissolve at practical research concentrations.

A clear, homogeneous solution is the target for any research preparation. A turbid, cloudy, or particulate preparation indicates incomplete dissolution; because the compound has not fully dispersed, the actual concentration in the preparation is uncertain and the preparation should not be used.

Common Reconstitution Solvents for Research Peptides

The choice of solvent affects both dissolution and the stability of the reconstituted preparation:

  • Bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol in water for injection) — the standard reconstitution solvent for most research peptides stocked by North Peptides UK. Suitable for the majority of hydrophilic peptides. The benzyl alcohol preservative allows the prepared solution to remain stable for laboratory research over a period of up to 28 days when refrigerated at 2–8°C. See the product page for availability.
  • Normal saline (0.9% NaCl, sterile) — used where benzyl alcohol is undesirable for a specific research model, or for compounds intended for intranasal research preparations such as Semax and Selank. No preservative, so preparations should be used within approximately 24 hours.
  • Dilute acetic acid (0.1–1% in water) — used for basic peptides that dissolve poorly in neutral water. Adding a trace of acid protonates basic residues, increasing aqueous solubility. Commonly useful for TB-500 and certain longer-chain peptides. Note that acetic acid alone is not a bacteriostatic medium — preparations made with it should be used promptly or diluted into bacteriostatic water after initial dissolution.
  • DMSO (dimethyl sulphoxide) — a small-molecule organic co-solvent occasionally used for highly hydrophobic peptides that resist aqueous dissolution. Not required for the compounds commonly stocked at North Peptides UK, where all listed peptides dissolve in aqueous media with appropriate technique.

Solubility Notes by Compound

The following notes cover the research compounds available from North Peptides UK:

  • BPC-157 — highly soluble in bacteriostatic water. Dissolves quickly and cleanly with minimal swirling. One of the most straightforward peptides to prepare in the laboratory.
  • TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4 fragment) — dissolves readily in bacteriostatic water for most research concentrations. At higher concentrations, adding a small volume of 0.1% acetic acid before diluting with bacteriostatic water assists dissolution. The compound is an actin-binding peptide; the sequence contains multiple basic residues, which accounts for its pH-sensitivity in aqueous media.
  • GHK-Cu — the copper chelate dissolves readily in bacteriostatic water or saline. Solutions are characteristically blue-green in colour; this is a normal property of the compound, not an indication of contamination or degradation. Among the most easily dissolved peptides in the catalogue.
  • KPV — a tri-peptide that is freely soluble in bacteriostatic water. Dissolves rapidly in small volumes.
  • Semax and Selank — both dissolve readily in saline or bacteriostatic water. For intranasal research model preparations, saline is typically used as the carrier. See the intranasal preparation guide for practical detail.
  • NAD+ — dissolves readily in bacteriostatic water. NAD+ is a dinucleotide rather than a peptide in the strict sense; it is highly hydrophilic and dissolves quickly.
  • Epitalon and Pinealon — both are short peptides (tetrapeptide and tripeptide respectively) and both freely soluble in bacteriostatic water.
  • Ipamorelin — generally soluble in bacteriostatic water. At higher preparation concentrations, beginning with a small volume of 0.1% acetic acid before diluting to final volume with bacteriostatic water may assist dissolution.
  • CJC-1295 — dissolves in bacteriostatic water. The compound is moderately hydrophilic; at higher concentrations, allow several minutes and gentle swirling rather than adding solvent and expecting immediate dissolution.
  • Retatrutide and Tirzepatide — both are long acylated peptides with a lipid moiety. They dissolve in bacteriostatic water, but the lipid component means the preparation requires patient swirling to achieve a homogeneous solution. Swirl gently and allow a few minutes; do not shake, which can cause aggregation.
  • SS-31 (Elamipretide) — dissolves readily in bacteriostatic water. A small, charged tetrapeptide that is freely soluble in aqueous media.

Troubleshooting: When a Compound Dissolves Slowly

If a research peptide does not dissolve readily using the standard approach, the following steps — applied in sequence — generally resolve the issue:

  1. Warm to room temperature — allow the vial to equilibrate fully before adding solvent. A cold vial can cause condensation and interfere with dissolution.
  2. Add solvent in stages — rather than adding the full target volume in one step, add a smaller volume, allow to dissolve, then add the remainder. This avoids overwhelming the solubility limit at high local concentration.
  3. Swirl, do not shake — vigorous shaking introduces air bubbles and mechanical stress that can cause peptide aggregation. Gentle rotary swirling, or inversion a few times, is sufficient for most peptides.
  4. Allow more time — some compounds, particularly lipidated or longer-chain peptides, dissolve slowly. Leave the vial at room temperature for 10–15 minutes, swirling occasionally, before concluding that dissolution has not occurred.
  5. Warm slightly — placing the sealed vial in a water bath at 30–37°C for a few minutes can improve dissolution rate for slow preparations. Do not apply direct heat or exceed 40°C.
  6. Try acetic acid first — for peptides prone to pH-dependent solubility (TB-500, Ipamorelin): dissolve the powder in a small volume of 0.1% acetic acid first, then bring to final volume with bacteriostatic water.

If a preparation remains turbid or particulate after all the above steps, the compound may have exceeded its solubility limit at the target concentration, or the vial may have a quality issue. Contact the North Peptides UK team on Telegram at @NORTHPEPTIDESUK for assistance.

Solubility and Research-Grade Purity

A compound that does not dissolve to produce a clear solution may not be a solubility problem — it may signal a quality issue. Insoluble particulate matter in a preparation that should dissolve readily can indicate the presence of impurities with different solubility profiles.

North Peptides UK lists all compounds with supplier-stated 99%+ purity, and independent HPLC laboratory reports are published on the lab reports page where available. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best solvent for reconstituting most research peptides?

Bacteriostatic water (0.9% benzyl alcohol) is the standard reconstitution solvent for most research peptides stocked by North Peptides UK. Its preservative content allows prepared solutions to remain stable for laboratory research for up to 28 days when refrigerated at 2–8°C.

What should I do if a research peptide will not fully dissolve?

Allow the vial to reach room temperature, add solvent in stages rather than all at once, and swirl gently rather than shaking. For basic peptides such as TB-500 or Ipamorelin, try dissolving in a small amount of 0.1% acetic acid first, then dilute to final volume with bacteriostatic water.

Why is the reconstituted solution from GHK-Cu blue-green in colour?

GHK-Cu is a copper chelate — the copper ion bound to the glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine tripeptide gives the solution its characteristic blue-green colour. This is a normal property of the compound, not a sign of contamination or degradation.

Can research peptides be used in humans?

No. All research compounds supplied by North Peptides UK are strictly for laboratory and scientific research and are not for human or animal consumption.
