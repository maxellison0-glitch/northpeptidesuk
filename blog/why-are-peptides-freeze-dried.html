Why Are Peptides Freeze-Dried?
Lyophilisation Explained

North Peptides UK · 5 min read · Research Use Only

If you have ordered research peptides, you will have received them as a fine powder in a sealed glass vial — not a liquid. That powder is a lyophilised (freeze-dried) material. This guide explains why that form exists, what the lyophilisation process involves, and what it means for storage and preparation in the laboratory.

What Is Lyophilisation?

Lyophilisation — commonly called freeze-drying — is a dehydration process used to remove water from a sample while preserving its molecular structure. It is the standard preservation method for biologics, peptides and proteins in pharmaceutical and research settings worldwide.

The process works in three stages:

  1. Freezing. The peptide solution is frozen, typically to temperatures between −40 °C and −80 °C. Water crystallises into ice.
  2. Primary drying (sublimation). Chamber pressure is reduced and gentle heat is applied. Ice converts directly from solid to vapour — bypassing the liquid phase entirely — and is drawn off. This removes the bulk of the water content.
  3. Secondary drying (desorption). Residual bound water molecules are removed by raising the temperature further under continued vacuum. The result is a dry, amorphous powder.

The defining advantage of sublimation over conventional evaporation is that the delicate molecular architecture of the peptide is not disrupted by a liquid-to-gas transition. The dry powder retains essentially the same structure as the original frozen solution.

Why Research Peptides Are Supplied as Lyophilised Powder

Peptides are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. In aqueous solution, those bonds are vulnerable to hydrolysis — water molecules can break the chain over time. Dissolved peptides are also susceptible to oxidation, aggregation and microbial growth. Any of these degradation pathways compromises sample integrity and makes research data unreliable.

Lyophilisation addresses all of these risks simultaneously:

  • Hydrolysis is halted. Without water, the peptide bonds are stable. Properly lyophilised peptides can remain chemically intact for months or years under correct storage conditions.
  • Oxidation is minimised. The low-water environment and inert-atmosphere vial sealing dramatically reduce oxidative degradation of sensitive residues.
  • Microbial growth cannot occur. Bacteria and fungi require water; a dry powder offers them nothing.
  • Purity is locked in at production. The purity verified by HPLC at manufacture is preserved in the powder until the researcher reconstitutes it for use.

Supplying research peptides in lyophilised form is therefore not a presentation preference — it is a scientific requirement for delivering a compound with documented, dependable properties to the laboratory.

Storing Lyophilised Research Peptides

Lyophilised peptides are significantly more stable than reconstituted solutions, but stability is not unconditional. The main threats to a lyophilised powder are moisture reabsorption, elevated temperature and light exposure.

FactorGuidanceWhy it matters
Temperature −20 °C long-term; 2–8 °C short-term Lower temperature slows all residual degradation pathways
Moisture Keep vial sealed; equilibrate to room temperature before opening Preventing condensation stops rehydration that restarts hydrolysis
Light Avoid direct light; store in a dark cabinet or original packaging UV and visible light can degrade aromatic amino acid residues
Freeze–thaw cycles Less critical for the dry powder; minimise for any reconstituted solution Repeated freeze–thaw cycles degrade reconstituted peptide far more than the lyophilised form

Reconstituting Lyophilised Peptides

Before a lyophilised research peptide can be used in a laboratory preparation, it must be dissolved in a suitable solvent. This is called reconstitution. The choice of solvent matters: it must be compatible with the compound, sterile, and appropriate for the intended research application.

Bacteriostatic water — sterile water containing 0.9% benzyl alcohol — is the standard reconstitution solvent for most research peptides. The benzyl alcohol acts as a preservative in the prepared solution, extending the working stability of the reconstituted material. Most research peptides dissolve cleanly and quickly in bacteriostatic water.

The general reconstitution procedure is:

  1. Allow the sealed vial to reach room temperature before opening — this prevents moisture condensation onto the powder.
  2. Draw the solvent into a sterile syringe.
  3. Inject the solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial, not directly onto the powder, to minimise mechanical disruption.
  4. Swirl gently to dissolve; do not shake the vial.
  5. Label the prepared vial with the preparation date.

The full procedure, solvent volumes and concentration arithmetic are in our peptide reconstitution guide.

Once reconstituted, store the solution at 2–8 °C, protect it from light and avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. With bacteriostatic water, most reconstituted solutions remain analytically stable for up to 28 days under refrigeration.

Lyophilisation and Purity Documentation

HPLC purity testing — the standard analytical method for research peptides — is almost always performed on the lyophilised material. A purity certificate that reports 99% reflects the composition of the freeze-dried powder before reconstitution. This is the most rigorous point at which to assess a compound's purity, because the dry material is at its most stable and homogeneous.

For researchers, this matters in a practical way: correct storage of the sealed vial preserves that certified purity right up to the point of reconstitution. Once the vial is opened and the powder dissolved, the researcher takes on the variables of solvent choice, preparation volume, storage temperature and time in solution.

North Peptides UK lists all compounds with supplier-stated purity at a minimum, and publishes independent third-party HPLC certificates for independently tested batches. Both are performed on the lyophilised material as supplied — the same form that arrives to a UK research laboratory.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do research peptides come as a powder rather than a liquid?

Because the lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder is far more stable than a solution. Without water, the peptide chains cannot hydrolyse, oxidise or aggregate as they would in aqueous solution. Supplying as a dry powder preserves the purity verified at manufacture until the researcher reconstitutes it for laboratory use.

How long do lyophilised peptides stay stable?

Under correct storage conditions — sealed at −20 °C, protected from moisture and light — most research peptides are stable for 12–24 months in lyophilised form. Once reconstituted, stability is shorter; with bacteriostatic water and refrigeration at 2–8 °C, most prepared solutions are analytically stable for up to 28 days.

Can the freeze-drying process damage a peptide?

Not under controlled conditions. Lyophilisation uses sublimation — ice converts directly to vapour, bypassing the liquid phase — which avoids the thermal and mechanical stresses that conventional evaporative drying would impose. It is the industry-standard method for preserving biologics and research peptides precisely because it preserves molecular integrity.

Can lyophilised peptides be used in humans?

No. All research peptides stocked by North Peptides UK — whether supplied as lyophilised powders or pre-reconstituted liquids — are for laboratory and scientific research only and are not for human or animal consumption.
