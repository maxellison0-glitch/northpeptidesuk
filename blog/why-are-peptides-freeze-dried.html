Why Are Peptides Freeze-Dried?
Lyophilisation Explained

North Peptides UK · 5 min read · Research Use Only

Almost every research peptide you will encounter is supplied as a lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder rather than a ready-made solution. This guide explains what lyophilisation is, why it is the standard for research-grade compounds, and what it means in practice for storage, reconstitution and laboratory handling.

What Is Lyophilisation?

Lyophilisation — also written freeze-drying — is a low-temperature dehydration process used to remove water from a sample while preserving its chemical structure. The process relies on sublimation: under high vacuum, ice converts directly to water vapour without passing through a liquid phase, which means the compound is never exposed to liquid water at elevated temperature.

The result is a dry, stable powder that retains the molecular structure of the original material. In pharmaceutical and research manufacture, lyophilisation is the established preservation method for sensitive biochemical compounds — including peptides — that would degrade rapidly if stored as an aqueous solution.

Why Research Peptides Are Supplied in Lyophilised Form

Peptides in aqueous solution are susceptible to several degradation pathways: hydrolysis can break peptide bonds over time, dissolved oxygen promotes oxidation of vulnerable amino acid side chains such as methionine and cysteine, and microbial contamination can propagate in any aqueous environment. Even at refrigerator temperatures, these processes continue — they are slowed, not stopped.

In lyophilised form, the absence of free water halts or dramatically slows all of these pathways. A correctly sealed and stored lyophilised peptide vial retains integrity at −20°C for extended periods — typically measured in years — and maintains usable stability at 2–8°C for many months in its dry state. This is why research-grade peptides are almost universally supplied as lyophilised powders rather than pre-made solutions.

The stability advantage is particularly relevant for researchers running multi-week programmes, where a consistent reference material is needed across multiple preparations. Lyophilisation ensures that each vial opened from a batch represents the same compound at a comparable quality.

How the Lyophilisation Process Works

A lyophilisation run proceeds in three defined stages:

  • Freezing. The prepared solution is cooled below its eutectic or glass-transition temperature. Water converts to ice and the peptide molecules are locked in a frozen matrix.
  • Primary drying (sublimation). Chamber pressure is reduced to well below the triple point of water. At this pressure, ice sublimes directly to vapour. Shelf temperature is carefully controlled to avoid melting the frozen product while maximising the sublimation rate. This stage removes the bulk of the water — typically over 95%.
  • Secondary drying (desorption). Shelf temperature is raised slightly and the vacuum is maintained to desorb residual bound water molecules remaining after primary drying. This final step reduces moisture content to the low levels — typically 1–3% — required for long-term stability.

The finished product is the white or off-white powder (sometimes called the "cake") visible inside a research vial. Appearance varies: some compounds form a compact, coherent cake; others yield a fine loose powder. This morphology reflects the compound's chemistry and any excipients used in manufacture, and does not indicate quality.

Reconstituting a Lyophilised Research Vial

Before a lyophilised compound can be used in a research preparation, it must be dissolved in an appropriate solvent. For most peptide preparations, bacteriostatic water is the standard choice: its 0.9% benzyl alcohol content inhibits microbial growth and extends the working life of the reconstituted solution to up to 28 days under refrigeration. For intranasal preparations, sterile 0.9% saline is used instead.

The abbreviated reconstitution procedure is:

  1. Allow the sealed vial to equilibrate to ambient temperature before opening, to prevent moisture condensation on the cold powder.
  2. Draw the chosen volume of bacteriostatic water into a clean syringe.
  3. Insert the needle through the rubber septum and direct the solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial — not directly onto the powder — to minimise foaming.
  4. Swirl the vial gently; do not shake vigorously. Most research peptides dissolve within a few minutes at ambient temperature.
  5. Label the vial with the preparation date and calculated concentration, then store at 2–8°C.

Full step-by-step instructions and concentration arithmetic are covered in the reconstitution guide. For compounds with lower intrinsic solubility, gentle warming of the solvent to approximately 37°C before addition can assist dissolution — compound-specific notes are included in that guide.

Storage Guidelines: Sealed Vials and Reconstituted Solutions

Lyophilised vials are stored at −20°C for the long term and at 2–8°C for shorter periods of regular use. The critical rule is to keep vials sealed and protected from moisture: even brief exposure to humid air allows water vapour to enter and begin re-dissolving the powder, which restarts the degradation clock. Vials should always be allowed to reach ambient temperature before opening, so that warm ambient air does not deposit condensation on the cold powder.

Once reconstituted, the peptide is again in aqueous solution and the same degradation pathways become active. Prepared solutions in bacteriostatic water are generally stored at 2–8°C, kept away from direct light, and not subjected to repeated freeze–thaw cycles. A working life of up to 28 days applies to most research peptides handled this way; compound-specific storage notes for all products stocked by North Peptides UK are included in the storage guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does lyophilised mean for a research peptide?

Lyophilised means freeze-dried: the compound has been dehydrated under vacuum to remove water content, yielding a stable dry powder. This form resists the degradation that would otherwise occur in an aqueous solution, making it the standard supply format for research-grade peptides.

Can lyophilised research peptides be used in humans?

No. Research peptides supplied by North Peptides UK are strictly for laboratory and scientific research and are not for human or animal consumption, regardless of form.

How long does a lyophilised vial stay stable?

When stored correctly at −20°C and kept sealed away from moisture, lyophilised research peptides maintain stability for extended periods, typically measured in years. Once reconstituted, the working solution should generally be used within 28 days when stored in bacteriostatic water at 2–8°C.

Why use bacteriostatic water rather than sterile water for reconstitution?

Bacteriostatic water contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol, which inhibits microbial growth and extends the stability of a reconstituted solution from a few hours (with sterile water alone) to up to 28 days under refrigeration. It is the standard choice for multi-use research vials.
