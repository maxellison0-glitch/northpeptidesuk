Why Are Peptides Freeze-Dried?
Lyophilisation Explained

North Peptides UK · 6 min read · Research Use Only

Almost every research peptide North Peptides UK supplies arrives as a white or off-white powder in a sealed glass vial. That powder is the result of lyophilisation — freeze-drying — a preservation process that removes moisture from the compound and locks it in a stable, dry form. This guide explains what lyophilisation is, why it is used for research peptides, and what it means in practice for handling in the laboratory. For research use only; not for human or animal consumption.

What Is Lyophilisation?

Lyophilisation — also written as lyophilization, and commonly called freeze-drying — is a dehydration process that removes water from a material under vacuum, without passing through a liquid phase. The material is first frozen, then exposed to a reduced-pressure environment in which the ice sublimes directly into vapour. The result is a dry, porous powder or cake that retains the molecular composition of the original material.

The process has three main stages:

  1. Freezing. The product is cooled to well below its eutectic temperature — typically −40°C or colder — to freeze all the water it contains.
  2. Primary drying (sublimation). Vacuum is applied and gentle heat is introduced. Ice sublimes directly to vapour, bypassing the liquid phase entirely. This stage removes the bulk of the water content.
  3. Secondary drying (desorption). Temperature is raised further to drive off bound moisture. When complete, the product typically contains less than 1–3% residual water.

The finished powder is then sealed into a glass vial — often under inert atmosphere — for storage and dispatch.

Why Research Peptides Are Supplied as Lyophilised Powder

Peptides are short chains of amino acids. As molecules they are relatively fragile in solution: water-based preparations degrade over time through hydrolysis (water breaking the peptide bond) and oxidation, and the rate of degradation is driven by temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, and metal-ion contamination. For research compounds that must be stored, transported, and used months after manufacture, a dry and stable form is strongly preferred over a liquid.

Lyophilisation addresses these stability challenges directly:

  • Moisture removal stops degradation. Water is the primary driver of hydrolysis and oxidation in peptide preparations. Removing it eliminates those pathways entirely.
  • Long-term stability. A lyophilised peptide stored at −20°C is typically stable for two to three years or longer — far exceeding the stability of an equivalent solution stored at the same temperature.
  • Shipping resilience. Many lyophilised peptides tolerate brief ambient-temperature transit without significant degradation, making distribution more practical than a liquid cold chain.
  • Purity is locked in. Because the process is conducted under controlled, inert conditions, the chemical composition of the peptide at the time of lyophilisation is preserved. A vial tested at 99% purity by HPLC should return the same result when retested after correct storage.

These properties make lyophilisation the standard method for supplying research-grade peptides commercially.

What to Expect When You Open the Vial

A lyophilised peptide vial typically contains a white or off-white powder or porous cake, depending on the peptide and the specific lyophilisation cycle used. Some compounds may have a distinct colour: GHK-Cu, for example, is a copper-chelating peptide that can have a blue tint to the powder, and a blue or blue-green solution is normal once reconstituted.

The vial may appear to contain very little material, even at larger masses. A 10 mg vial of a high-molecular-weight peptide can produce a cake that barely covers the base of the vial — that is normal and does not indicate an underfilled product. Differences in cake structure (fine powder vs. porous solid) reflect differences in the lyophilisation cycle and the excipient formulation, not in purity or potency.

Lyophilised Powder vs. Pre-Reconstituted Liquid

Some research compounds are available in both a lyophilised form and as a pre-reconstituted liquid (pen vials, for example). The choice between them involves a trade-off between long-term stability and convenience of preparation:

Property Lyophilised powder Pre-reconstituted liquid
Long-term stabilityExcellent — years at −20°C when sealedMonths at 2–8°C
Shipping resilienceCan tolerate brief ambient transitRequires cold chain

For most research settings where compounds will be stocked and used over weeks or months, lyophilised powder is the more stable and cost-effective option. A pre-reconstituted format may be preferred where speed of preparation is a priority and the compound will be used promptly.

Reconstituting Lyophilised Peptides in the Laboratory

Before use in a research preparation, a lyophilised peptide is reconstituted — dissolved into a suitable aqueous solvent to create a working solution. The most commonly used solvent for research peptides is bacteriostatic water (sterile water containing 0.9% benzyl alcohol as a preservative), which is suitable for most compounds and extends the working life of the reconstituted solution compared with plain sterile water. A full guide to reconstitution including concentration arithmetic is at our reconstitution guide.

Key procedure steps:

  1. Allow the sealed vial to reach room temperature before opening, to prevent condensation on the powder.
  2. Draw the target volume of bacteriostatic water into a clean syringe.
  3. Run the solvent slowly down the inside wall of the vial — do not aim the stream directly at the powder cake, as this can cause foaming.
  4. Swirl gently; do not shake. Vigorous agitation can cause aggregation and is not necessary — most peptides dissolve within a few minutes at room temperature.
  5. Label the vial with the compound name, concentration, and preparation date before storing.

For compound-specific solubility notes — some peptides dissolve better in dilute acetic acid or require co-solvents — see our guide to peptide solubility.

Storage of Lyophilised Vials

To preserve the stability that lyophilisation creates, storage conditions matter:

  • Sealed (unopened) vials: −20°C for long-term storage, protected from light. 2–8°C is acceptable for vials that will be used within days.
  • Seal integrity: The vial should remain sealed until the researcher is ready to reconstitute. Once the seal is broken, ambient humidity can begin to re-hydrate the powder — starting the same degradation processes lyophilisation was designed to prevent.
  • Reconstituted solutions: Refrigerate at 2–8°C, protect from light, and avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. With bacteriostatic water, a reconstituted solution typically remains stable for up to 28 days.

A complete reference on storage conditions — including compound-by-compound notes — is at How to Store Research Peptides Correctly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does lyophilised mean?

Lyophilised means freeze-dried. It describes a compound that has had its water removed under vacuum via sublimation (ice converting directly to vapour), producing a dry powder that preserves the molecular composition of the original material.

Why are research peptides supplied as powder rather than a liquid?

Lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder is far more stable than a liquid solution. Removing moisture stops hydrolysis and oxidation, allowing the compound to be stored for years at −20°C with minimal degradation. Liquid solutions degrade faster and have a shorter working life.

Can lyophilised research peptides be used in humans?

No. Research peptides supplied by North Peptides UK are for laboratory and scientific research only and are not for human or animal consumption. They are not medicines, are not licensed for any clinical use, and are supplied strictly as reference research materials.

How do I dissolve (reconstitute) a lyophilised peptide?

Add bacteriostatic water slowly down the inside wall of the vial, then swirl gently. Do not shake. Most peptides dissolve within a few minutes at room temperature.
