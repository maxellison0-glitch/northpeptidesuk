Why Are Peptides Freeze-Dried?
Lyophilisation Explained

North Peptides UK · 5 min read · Research Use Only

Most research peptides arrive as a fine, white lyophilised (freeze-dried) powder inside a sealed vial — not as a ready-to-use liquid. Understanding why, and what it means for how you prepare and store your compounds, is fundamental to working with peptides in a laboratory setting.

What Is Lyophilisation?

Lyophilisation — commonly called freeze-drying — is a dehydration process in which water is removed from a substance under vacuum after the substance has been frozen. Rather than evaporating from a liquid state, the water passes directly from ice to vapour through a physical process called sublimation. The result is a dry, porous solid that retains the chemical structure of the original compound without the degradation that liquid-phase drying would cause.

The technique was developed during the Second World War to stabilise penicillin and blood plasma for field use, and today it is the standard method for preserving delicate biological molecules — including synthetic peptides — for long-term storage and research applications.

Why Peptides Are Supplied in Lyophilised Form

Peptides are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. In aqueous solution, those bonds are susceptible to hydrolysis — gradual breakdown driven by water molecules. In solution, peptides are also vulnerable to oxidation and microbial degradation. Removing water slows all three processes substantially, extending the useful shelf-life of the compound from days or weeks (as a reconstituted solution) to months or years when stored correctly as a powder.

A second practical benefit is shipping stability. A frozen solution can thaw during transit; lyophilised powder is stable at ambient temperature for short periods, which simplifies logistics and reduces cold-chain risk. Once received, the researcher reconstitutes only the volume needed for a given experiment, leaving the remainder of the powder sealed and stable.

For compounds like BPC-157, GHK-Cu, and TB-500 — peptides valued in tissue-repair and gastrointestinal research models — lyophilisation is well suited: the porous cake structure reconstitutes quickly and evenly when the appropriate solvent is added.

The Lyophilisation Process

Industrial lyophilisation takes place in three stages:

  1. Freezing. The peptide solution is cooled to a temperature at which all free water is solidified. The exact temperature varies by compound and formulation; typical values are −40°C to −80°C.
  2. Primary drying (sublimation). Chamber pressure is reduced to a high vacuum and a controlled amount of heat is applied. Frozen water sublimes directly to vapour, which is captured on a condenser. This stage removes the bulk of the water and accounts for most of the cycle time — often 24 to 48 hours per batch.
  3. Secondary drying (desorption). Temperature is raised further at low pressure to remove residual bound water. The target is a final moisture content typically below 1–3%, at which point the compound is stable for long-term storage.

The vial is then sealed under inert gas or vacuum with a crimped rubber stopper, protecting the lyophilised cake from atmospheric moisture until the researcher is ready to prepare a working solution.

What Lyophilised Peptide Powder Looks Like

Lyophilised peptide powder is typically a white or off-white porous cake or loose powder. It may appear as a solid disc clinging to the sides of the vial, or as a fine powder settled at the bottom, depending on the volume and fill formulation used in the lyophilisation cycle. Both presentations are normal and do not indicate degradation.

Some peptides produce a very small pellet — this is common for high-purity, low-volume fills where the total mass of peptide is just a few milligrams. Others fill a larger portion of the vial. In all cases, the powder should be dry and non-tacky (when tapped against the glass), free of discolouration, and sealed with no visible moisture condensation inside the vial.

A pale yellow or slightly off-white colour in the powder is normal for certain peptides with aromatic or copper-containing residues (GHK-Cu, for example, may carry a slight blue tint from the copper ion). These are expected characteristics of the compound, not signs of contamination.

Reconstituting Freeze-Dried Peptides

To prepare a working solution, a solvent is drawn into a syringe and introduced into the sealed vial. Bacteriostatic water is the standard choice for most research peptides: it is sterile, isotonic, and contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol as a preservative, which extends the stability of the prepared solution relative to plain sterile water. For compounds with lower water solubility, dilute acetic acid (0.1–1% v/v) or DMSO may be used as a co-solvent.

The correct technique: run the solvent down the inner wall of the vial rather than directing it straight onto the powder cake. Swirl gently to dissolve — do not vortex or shake vigorously, as mechanical agitation at this stage can disrupt the peptide structure. Most peptides dissolve within one to two minutes. Label the vial with the compound name and preparation date, then store as directed for the compound.

Storage of Lyophilised and Reconstituted Peptides

Lyophilised (un-reconstituted) peptides should be stored frozen at −20°C or below, protected from light and moisture. Most research peptides remain stable under these conditions for at least 12–24 months from the manufacture date. Avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles on un-reconstituted vials — if the powder is not required immediately, keep it frozen and remove the vial only when ready to prepare a working solution.

Once reconstituted with bacteriostatic water, refrigerate the solution at 2–8°C and use within 28 days. Do not refreeze a reconstituted vial. If plain sterile water is used instead of bacteriostatic water, stability is shorter — typically 7–14 days when refrigerated — because there is no benzyl alcohol preservative. Detailed guidance on storage conditions for specific compounds is in the peptide storage guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does lyophilised mean?

Lyophilised means freeze-dried. The term refers to a compound from which water has been removed by sublimation under vacuum, leaving a dry powder or cake that is stable for long-term storage. It is the standard supply format for research peptides.

Are freeze-dried peptides more stable than peptides in solution?

Yes, significantly. In solution, peptide bonds are exposed to hydrolysis, oxidation, and microbial degradation. Lyophilised powder, sealed and stored frozen, is protected from all three processes — shelf-life extends from days or weeks to months or years.

How do I reconstitute a lyophilised peptide?

Add bacteriostatic water (or the appropriate co-solvent for the compound) by running it down the inside wall of the vial. Swirl gently until the powder dissolves — do not shake. Label the vial with the preparation date and refrigerate. The full procedure and concentration calculations are in the reconstitution guide.
